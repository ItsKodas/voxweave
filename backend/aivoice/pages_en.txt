# The Lighthouse Keeper
For thirty-one years, Margaret Hale kept the lighthouse at the edge of Gull Point. Every evening at dusk she climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps, wiped the salt from the great glass lens, and lit the lamp that guided fishing boats safely home.

Most nights were quiet. She would sit by the window with a mug of strong tea, listening to the waves crash against the rocks below. Sometimes she read old adventure novels. Sometimes she simply watched the sky change from orange to purple to a deep, velvety black.

But one stormy night in November, everything changed. The wind howled like an angry animal, and rain hammered against the tower so hard that the whole building seemed to shiver. Just after midnight, Margaret noticed a faint light flickering far out at sea. It blinked once, twice, then vanished.

"That's no fishing boat," she whispered to herself. "Somebody's in trouble."

She grabbed her heavy yellow coat and the radio from the kitchen shelf. Her fingers were trembling as she turned the dial. At first she heard nothing but crackling static. Then, very faintly, a young man's voice broke through.

"Hello? Is anyone there? Our engine has failed, and we're drifting toward the rocks!"

Margaret took a deep breath and pressed the button. "This is the Gull Point lighthouse. I can see you. Stay calm, keep your lights on, and listen carefully. I'm going to guide you around the reef."

For nearly an hour, she talked them through the darkness. Left a little. Now straight ahead. Hold steady. Wait for the next wave. Her voice never rose, even when her heart was pounding.

At last, as the first grey light of morning crept over the horizon, the little boat slipped into the calm water of the harbour. Three exhausted sailors waved up at the tower, and Margaret, laughing with relief, waved back.

The next week, a thank-you card arrived in the post. Inside was a photograph of the three sailors, grinning, and a short note: "To the voice in the storm. We owe you everything."

Margaret pinned it beside the window, where she could see it every night.

# Weekend Plans
So, what are you actually doing this weekend? Because honestly, I've got no idea what I'm doing yet.

My original plan was to stay home, order a pizza, and finally finish that series everyone keeps talking about. You know the one, with the detectives and the weird house in the woods? I'm about halfway through, and I keep falling asleep before the good part. Every single time!

But then my sister called on Thursday. She said, "Why don't we go camping? The weather's supposed to be perfect." And I said, "Camping? In October? Are you serious?" She just laughed and said I'd be fine if I packed enough socks.

I'm not convinced. The last time we went camping, it rained for two days straight. The tent leaked, the marshmallows got soggy, and we spent most of Saturday playing cards in the car. Actually, that part was pretty fun. I won almost every game, which she still refuses to admit.

Anyway, I told her I'd think about it. Then my friend Jordan messaged me about a concert on Saturday night. Apparently, a band we loved in school is playing a reunion show downtown. Tickets are cheap, and the venue's only twenty minutes away. How could I possibly say no to that?

So now I'm stuck. Do I go camping with my sister, go to the concert with Jordan, or just stay on the couch with my pizza and my detectives? Decisions, decisions.

Okay, here's what I'm thinking. Friday night, pizza and the series, no matter what. Saturday morning, I'll drive out to the campsite, help set up, and maybe go for a hike by the lake. Then Saturday evening, I'll head back for the concert. Sunday, I'll sleep in until noon and do absolutely nothing.

Is that too ambitious? Probably. Will I end up exhausted? Definitely. But you only live once, right?

What about you, though? Any big plans? Please tell me you're doing something more relaxing than I am.

# How Bread Rises
Bread is one of the oldest foods people make, and yet the science behind it is surprisingly fascinating. At its simplest, bread needs only four ingredients: flour, water, salt, and yeast. What happens when you mix them together is a small chemical miracle.

Let's start with the flour. Wheat flour contains two important proteins called glutenin and gliadin. When you add water and begin to knead the dough, these proteins link together to form gluten, a stretchy network that behaves a bit like a web of tiny elastic bands. The longer you knead, the stronger and smoother that network becomes.

Next comes the yeast. Yeast is a living, single-celled fungus. When it's mixed into warm, damp dough, it wakes up and starts feeding on the sugars in the flour. As it eats, it produces two things: alcohol and carbon dioxide gas. The gas forms thousands of tiny bubbles, and the gluten network traps them. That is why the dough slowly puffs up, sometimes doubling in size within an hour or two.

Temperature matters enormously. Yeast works best at around twenty-five to thirty degrees Celsius. If the kitchen is too cold, the dough rises very slowly. If it's too hot, above roughly sixty degrees, the yeast dies completely. Many bakers actually prefer a long, cool rise overnight in the fridge, because it develops a deeper, more complex flavour.

Salt plays a quieter role. It strengthens the gluten, controls how quickly the yeast works, and, of course, makes the bread taste good. Without salt, bread tastes flat and oddly sweet.

Finally, there's the oven. In the first few minutes of baking, the heat makes the trapped gas expand rapidly, and the loaf grows one last time. Bakers call this oven spring. Then the proteins set, the starches firm up, and the outside turns golden brown through a process known as the Maillard reaction, the same reaction that browns toast, roasted coffee, and grilled vegetables.

So the next time you tear open a warm, crusty loaf, remember: you're holding the result of biology, chemistry, and physics, all working together in perfect harmony.

# A Letter to an Old Friend
Dear Sam,

I've started this letter four times now, and every time I've crossed it out and begun again. It's strange, isn't it? We used to talk for hours without ever running out of things to say, and now I can barely manage a first sentence.

It's been almost six years since you moved away. I still remember the morning you left. Your car was so full of boxes that you couldn't see out of the back window, and you'd tied a lamp to the roof with a piece of blue rope. I laughed so hard I nearly cried. Then I actually cried, once you'd turned the corner.

So much has happened since then. I finally changed jobs. I work at a small bookshop now, the one on Mill Street with the creaky wooden floors and the cat who sleeps in the poetry section. I adopted a dog, a scruffy little terrier named Biscuit, who is afraid of pigeons but somehow not of thunderstorms. And I learned to cook, properly this time. You'd be shocked. I can even make that lemon cake your grandmother used to bake.

But I've also lost touch with too many people, and you're the one I miss the most.

Do you remember the summer we decided to walk the entire coastal path? We were so sure we'd finish it in a week. By the third day, our feet were covered in blisters, we'd run out of snacks, and we'd taken at least a dozen wrong turns. And still, it's one of my happiest memories. We sat on that cliff at sunset, eating squashed sandwiches, talking about everything we were going to do with our lives.

I wonder sometimes whether we did any of it. I wonder whether you're happy.

I'm not writing because I want anything. I just wanted you to know that I think of you often, that I'm grateful for every ridiculous adventure we shared, and that the door is always open. If you're ever back in town, the kettle's on, Biscuit will greet you loudly, and I'll make the lemon cake.

Write back when you can. Or call. Or just turn up. Any of those would make my whole year.

With love, always,
Alex

# Game Night
Alright, everybody, settle down, settle down! Welcome to the most competitive game night this living room has ever seen. Snacks are on the table, drinks are in the fridge, and the rules are taped to the wall, so nobody can claim they didn't know.

Tonight's first game is a quiz. Three rounds: history, science, and absolutely random nonsense. Each correct answer is worth one point. Get a question wrong, and you lose half a point. Shout out the answer before I've finished reading the question, and you lose a whole point. Yes, Danny, that rule is specifically because of you.

Ready? Here we go. Round one, history. Question one: in which year did the first person walk on the moon? Anyone? Nineteen sixty-nine! Correct, well done, Priya, that's one point to the blue team.

Question two: which ancient wonder stood in the harbour of Rhodes? Danny? No, it was not a giant sandwich. It was the Colossus. Half a point off, I'm sorry, those are the rules.

Okay, round two, science. How many bones are there in the adult human body? Two hundred and six! Brilliant. What's the chemical symbol for gold? Au, exactly. And what's the fastest land animal? The cheetah, of course. Who said ostrich? Come on, really?

And now, everybody's favourite, round three: absolute nonsense. Question one: how many hot dogs can a person eat in ten minutes? Nobody knows? The world record is seventy-six. Disgusting, but impressive. Question two: what colour is a polar bear's skin? Not white! It's black. Their fur just looks white. I know, my mind was blown too.

Final scores, then. The red team has eleven and a half points. The blue team has, wait for it, twelve! Blue team wins by half a point! Can you believe it? Half a point!

Danny, I can see you shaking your head. Don't worry, there's still the card game, the drawing game, and the dreaded charades. The night is young, my friends, and anything could happen.

Now, who wants more popcorn before round two begins?
